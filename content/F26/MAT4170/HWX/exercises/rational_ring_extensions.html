    1. [0] Show that \(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \in \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}]\). (This is "obvious".)
    2. [3] Let \(\gamma = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3\). Find a degree-\(4\) polynomial in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\) with a root of gamma, using the fact that \begin{align*} \gamma^1 &= \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3 \\ \gamma^2 &= 2 \sqrt{6}+5 \\ \gamma^3 &= 11 \sqrt{2}+9 \sqrt{3} \\ \gamma^4 &= 20 \sqrt{6}+49. \end{align*} (Note: this is called the minimal polynomial of \(\gamma\), and \(\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\) is called a degree-\(4\) extension of \(\mathbb{Q}\).)
    3. [1] Explain why every element of \(\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\) can be written in the form \[ a_0 + a_1\gamma + a_2\gamma^2 + a_3\gamma^3. \]
    4. [2] Show that \(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \in \mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\) by finding rational coefficients such that \begin{align*} \sqrt{2} &= a_0 + a_1\gamma + a_2\gamma^2 + a_3\gamma^3 \\ \sqrt{3} &= a'_0 + a'_1\gamma + a'_2\gamma^2 + a'_3\gamma^3. \end{align*}
