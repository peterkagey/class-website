For a two week period between 11–13, you will be working on learning a topic of your choosing, and writing a homework-style question (or a set of questions) guiding another student through what you learned.

My experiences writing exercises—and learning a lot every time I do—is the motivation for this assignment. I hope that you will write exercises that will be interesting to your classmates and to future abstract algebra students, and I expect you'll learn a lot along the way.

Each group will choose a topic and work together to learn about that topic. Then each student in the group will write their own multi-part exercise guiding other students through that topic. The exercises that you and your group mates write can be overlapping, but should not be identical.

The exercise should be worth approximately 10 points, meaning that you expect it would take your classmate roughly 60–100 minutes to complete. Each student will also write up a short reflection and along with the solution to their exercises.

Guidelines

You will work with a group to research a topic in abstract algebra, and then every member of your group will individually write a challenging "10 point" exercise on that topic. The goal is to write an exercise that will guide your classmates through new ideas, that you think they could finish in about 100 minutes. This exercise should be self-contained, perhaps including a definition or proposition, and having your classmate work through a computation, proof, and/or example.

You cannot use LLM tools to generate any part of the text in your exercise. (On your final draft only, you can use a tool like Grammarly to check grammar, etc, but you need to implement any suggested changes yourself.)

You will submit a rough draft to Gradescope by Thursday, November 12th. This draft should both showcase the progress you have made writing the problem, and it should outline any ideas you are planning to implement.

Sections

Write your exercise up in \(\LaTeX\) in three sections: a reflection, the exercise, and solution writeup.

Reflection

The first \section{Reflection} will include 5+ sentences discussing what you learned while writing this exercise, how the other students in your group contributed to the exercise or your understanding of it, your goals while writing the exercise, what you might want to explore if you were to write another exercise, or anything else that might be related to your experience writing the exercise.

Also, please answer the following questions:

  • Can use the exercise you have written this term or in future terms?
  • If so, how would you like to be attributed? (e.g., your full name, your first name, your initials, anonymously?)
  • Can I make changes/edits to your exercise (with attribution) if I give it to future students?

Exercise

The second \section{Exercise} will contain your "10+ point" exercise write up exploring an idea that we haven't seen in class, or revisiting an idea from class with a different perspective. Be mindful to provide or guide the prospective student through rich examples. You might also choose to give hints or other ways of helping the student avoid getting stuck.

I encourage you to use \begin{enumerate}...\end{enumerate} for the sub-parts of your code.

Solution

The final \section{Solution} should contain an exemplary write-up of the solution your exercise.

Possible topics

The following is a list of possible topics you could explore. You are encouraged to choose a topic that is not on this list.

Burnside's Lemma

Work in depth on a problem to count a family of objects using Burnside's Lemma, and perhaps add a new entry to the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences.

Latin Squares

Cayley tables have the "Latin Square" property, which means that they behave a bit like Sudoku. Talk about Latin squares and their algebraic properties. Does every Latin Square correspond to a group?

Classification of Finite Simple Groups

In the 3Blue1Brown video we watched at the beginning of the term, Grant Sanderson talked about one of humanity's greatest intellectual accomplishments: the classification of finite simple groups. What are finite simple groups? Describe some of the groups in this family, such as the alternating group \(A_n\) or the finite simple groups of Lie type.

The Fundamental Group of a Topological Space

Groups show up in topology is a really cool way, where the elements of the group are certain kinds of paths on the space. Learn about these groups and their connection to the free group.

Polytopes as Cayley Graphs

We saw that Cayley graphs sometimes look like cubes (\(D_4\)), like hexagons (\(C_6\)), and even like a truncated octahedron (\(S_4\)). What other shapes arise from Cayley graphs?

Semidirect Products

One topic that we didn't explore in class is that of (outer) semidirect products, which allow you to write \(D_n = C_n \rtimes C_2\). Describe how we can use these to combine two smaller groups into a new interesting group, such as the group of translations and \(90^\circ\) rotations of the plane, \(\mathbb{Z}^2 \rtimes C_4\).

Lamplighter Group

Can we come up with a group \(G = \langle S \mid R\rangle\) where \(S\) is the set of generators and \(R\) is the set of relations, but where \(S\) can be finite and \(R\) must be infinite? ("Finitely generated but not finitely related") The famous example of this is a cool application of wreath products.

Other Algebraic Structures

We've have (or will) study groups, abelian groups, rings, fields, and integral domains, but this term we won't talk about modules, vector spaces, algebras, semigroups, quasigroups, lattices, monoids, loops, and magmas. Tell us about one or more of these ideas.

Quaternion Group

Tell us more about the quaternion group, and maybe about how it generalizes into the octonions, and sedenions.

Frieze and Wallpaper Groups

There are 17 "wallpaper groups" and 7 "frieze groups" which are groups of repeating 2-dimensional patterns. Describe them, find as many examples of each as you can "in the wild" and show how they are groups.

Matrix Groups

Tell us about the general linear group, special linear group, and projective linear group and how they can be used in robotics and other applications.

Word Problem for Groups

Given a group presentation \(G = \langle S \mid R\rangle\) and a word with letters in \(S\), is that word the identity? This problem is unsolvable in general, meaning that there is no general purpose algorithm for solving this problem. What's going on with this?

Coxeter Groups

We can understand the symmetries of an object in space in terms of reflections or kaleidoscopes. Tell us about some of these, perhaps focusing on the icosahedral group.

Sylow Theorems

Sure you believe me when I tell you that there are five groups of order \(8\) up to isomorphism, but how do you prove it? You use the Sylow theorems!

Elliptic Curves

You can define a group on Elliptic curves, which are used in Elliptic Curve Cryptography and in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. What does this group look like?

Symmetries of Graphs (Graph Theory)

The symmetries of an graph form a group, called the automorphism group of the graph. Given a group, can you construct a graph whose symmetries are isomorphic to that group?

Automorphism group

Given a group \(G\), we can build a group out of all of the isomorphisms \[ \operatorname{Aut}(G) = \{\phi \colon G \to G \mid \phi \text{ is an isomorphism}\}, \] where the binary operation is function composition. Give examples of groups and understand their automorphism groups.

Exceptional Outer Automorphism of \(S_6\)

In particular the symmetric group \(S_6\) has a very interesting automorphism. Look at the exceptional outer automorphism of \(S_6\), and check out Travis R. Taylor's cool explainer post.

Galois theory

Galois theory tells us about the symmetry of roots of polynomials. Did you know that in any arithmetic equation (i.e. addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division) involving rational numbers and \(\sqrt{2}\), you can replace every instance of \(\sqrt{2}\) with \(-\sqrt{2}\), and the statement is still true? For instance, if you know that \[ \frac{ 2 + \sqrt{2} }{3 - \sqrt{2}^3} = 10 + 7\sqrt{2}, \] then it must also be true that \[ \frac{ 2 + (-\sqrt{2}) }{3 - (-\sqrt{2})^3} = 10 + 7(-\sqrt{2}), \] and this fact is related to field automorphisms.

The Weyl algebra (the ring of differential operators with polynomial coefficients). It admits a quite concrete description and there are many interesting things to say about it.

Exterior algebra of a vector space

Another elementary ring to consider is the Exterior Algebra of a vector space. That has the advantage of having both commutative and non-commutative parts, and having a relationship to linear algebra via determinants.
