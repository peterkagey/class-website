1. As formally or as informally as you'd like, prove that if \(g\) is an element of a group with a finite order \(|g| = k\), then the order of the subgroup generated by \(g\) is also \(k\), namely \(|\langle g \rangle| = k\).
  2. A cube has \(24\) rotational symmetries (i.e., non mirror-image symmetries), and we call this symmetry group \(O\) for "(rotational) octahedral symmetry." What are the possible orders of subgroups of \(O\)? (e.g., can there exist an order-\(7\) subgroup of \(O\)?)
  3. Suppose that I have three hexagons on the table: one red, one green, and one blue. Every time I leave the classroom, and a student comes up and applies the same symmetry \((g_1, g_2, g_3) \in D_6 \times D_6 \times D_6\) to them. Prove that the 12th time I enter the classroom, the hexagons are precisely in their starting position.
  4. Bézout's identity tells us that the elements of \((\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times\) are precisely the positive integers \(1 \leq k < n\) that are coprime to \(n\), meaning that \(\gcd(n,k)=1\).
    Euler's phi function \(\phi(n)\) counts the positive integers less than \(n\) that are coprime to \(n\).
    Euler's Theorem tells us that if \(a\) is coprime to \(n\) then \[ a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n. \]
    1. Use your work from last week to show that \(\phi(24) = 8\), and use Euler's theorem to compute \[ 13^9 = 10604499373 \pmod{24}. \]
