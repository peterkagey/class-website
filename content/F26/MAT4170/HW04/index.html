Exercise 4 [2] (Associativity) Do four of the following six parts. You can do the remaining two for one point each. Tag them under the final choice exercise; they will not count toward the ≤7 point cap—but if you go over that ≤7 point cap, remind me in the final choice exercise that I said this!
  1. With the exception of HW0 Choice Exercise 8.2b, we haven't really proven that a binary operation is associative. Prove or disprove whether the following binary operations (inspired by Pinter's Abstract Algebra) are associative on the \(X\). If the operation is not associative, give a counter-example. If it is associative, determine whether or not the operation has a (two-sided) identity element in \(X\).
    1. Let \(X = \mathbb{Z}\) and let \(a \oplus b = a + b + 1\).
    2. Let \(X = \{1,2,3,\dots, 919\}\) and let \(a \ominus b = b\).
    3. Let \(X = \mathbb{Z}\) and let \(a \boxplus b = |a + b|\)
    4. Let \(X = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}\) and let \[ (a,b) \boxminus (c,d) = (ac-bd, ad + bc) \] (Do you recognize what this secretly is?)
    5. Let \(X = \{-4,-2,-1,0,3,7,8\}\) and let \(a \mathbin\lozenge b = \min(a,b)\)
    6. Let \(X = \mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}\) and let \(a \otimes b = \frac{1}{ab}\)
Exercise 5 [4] (Applying Lagrange's theorem)
  1. As formally or as informally as you'd like, prove that if \(g\) is an element of a group with a finite order \(|g| = k\), then the order of the subgroup generated by \(g\) is also \(k\), namely \(|\langle g \rangle| = k\).
  2. A cube has \(24\) rotational symmetries (i.e. non mirror-image symmetries), and we call this symmetry group \(O\) for "(rotational) octahedral symmetry." What are the possible orders of subgroups of \(O\)? (e.g., can there exist an order-\(7\) subgroup of \(O\)?)
  3. Suppose that I have three hexagons on the table: one red, one green, and one blue. Every time I leave the classroom, and a student comes up and applies the same symmetry \(g_1, g_2, g_3 \in D_6 \times D_6 \times D_6\) to them. Prove that the 12th time I enter the classroom, the hexagons are precisely in their starting position.
  4. Bézout's identity tells us that the elements of \((\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times\) are precisely the positive integers \(1 \leq k < n\) that are coprime to \(n\), meaning that \(\gcd(n,k)=1\).
    Euler's phi function \(\phi(n)\) counts the positive integers less than \(n\) that are coprime to \(n\).
    Euler's Theorem tells us that if \(a\) is coprime to \(n\) then \[ a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n. \]
    1. Use your work from last week to show that \(\phi(24) = 8\), and use Lagrange's theorem to compute \(13^9 = 10604499373 \pmod 24\).
    2. Use the facts from the previous parts (together with theorems you know and love from class) to prove Euler's Theorem! (This was my favorite proof in undergrad! 😊)
Exercise 6 [4] (Cosets and equivalence relations)
  1. [2] Let \(H = \langle r^2, r^3f \rangle \leq D_6\).
    1. Explicitly write all of the elements of \(H\).
    2. Explicitly write all of the (left) cosets of \(H\), giving at least two possible names for each coset.
    3. Explain what it means for the cosets to partition \(D_6\).
    4. [2] Let \((G,*)\) be a group and \(H \leq G\) be a subgroup. Suppose that \(a \sim b\) if and only if there exists \(h \in H\) such that \(a = b*h\). We showed in class that this relation is symmetric and reflexive. Show that the relation is also transitive (and thus an equivalence relation.)
