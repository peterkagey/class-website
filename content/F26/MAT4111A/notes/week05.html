We say that \(a\) is congruent to \(b\) modulo \(n\), written \[ a \equiv b \pmod n, \] if \(a - b = kn\) for some integer \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\).

The formal definition is helpful for proving things, but also keep in mind the informal definition that "two numbers are congruent mod \(n\) if they have the same remainder on division by \(n\)."

If \(a \equiv a' \pmod n\) and \(b \equiv b' \pmod n\), then \[ a + b \equiv a' + b' \pmod n \] and \[ a \times b \equiv a' \times b' \pmod n. \]

This means that we can do convenient replacements: for instance, since \[ 71 \equiv -1 \pmod{72}, \] then \[ 71 \times 14 \equiv -1 \times 14 \equiv -14 \equiv 58 \pmod{72}. \]

Also note that modular arithmetic can help with divisibility problems: we say that \({n \mid a}\) (read "\(n\) divides \(a\)" which means "\(a\) is divisible by \(n\)") if and only if \({a \equiv 0 \pmod n}\).

Chinese remainder theorem

See Evan Chen's 2014 article on The Chinese Remainder Theorem.
The Chinese remainder theorem states that if $m_1, m_2, \dots m_k$ are pairwise coprime (i.e., $\gcd(m_i, m_j) = 1$ for all $i \neq j$) then for all $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_i,$ the system of congruences \[ \begin{align*} n \equiv &\ r_1 \pmod{m_1} \\ n \equiv &\ r_2 \pmod{m_2} \\ \ & \vdots \\ n \equiv &\ r_k \pmod{m_k} \end{align*} \] has a unique solution for $n$ modulo $m_1m_2\cdots m_k$.

Euler's theorem

Euler's theorem allows us to deal with powers in modular arithmetic, in certain circumstances.

Euler's phi function \(\phi(n)\) counts the number of positive integers less than \(n\) that are coprime to \(n\) (i.e., share no common factors.)
Euler's theorem says that if \(a\) is coprime to \(n\), then \[ a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n. \]
